Forge: Compact World-Model Systems Forged for One Use Case

Khashayar Mansourizadeh and Ayda Golahmadi

Elentaria, Netherlands · Correspondence: khashayar@elentaria.ai

Judge Benchmark v1 results Localization results Cite Announcement

Abstract

Most of the field expects capability to come from ever larger homogeneous models. We take the other road. Forge is a method for building a compact system of specialized parts for one use case, forged around a domain world model that carries long-term memory, understands the domain, and predicts the consequences of actions before they are taken. The parts do not need to share an architecture; they are connected through trained connectors and each acts as an organ with one job. We call the resulting architecture MALWIM: Multi-Stage Agentic Loops With Inhomogeneous Models.

This report describes the first instance, built for software engineering, and presents the first measured results of its organs. The core organ, the Predictor, is a 137-million-parameter coding world model that scores a proposed code change against the issue it is meant to fix, without running any test: much as a person weighs the consequences of an action in their mind before acting in the world. That judgement is what lets the system iterate and refine its proposals until it reaches the best outcome it can. On a benchmark of 100 test-verified GitHub issues and 938 candidate changes, it ranks third of 15 judges by balanced accuracy, tied with Gemini 3.8 Flash and behind Claude Opus 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol, and first of all 15 at rejecting broken versions of a fix. The whole system stays under one billion parameters, runs on a single workstation GPU, and was built by two co-founders for under $15,000.

CHAPTER 1

Forging and MALWIM

A large language model is one homogeneous network asked to do everything: remember, understand, plan, write, and check. It does all of it adequately and none of it efficiently, and its size makes it expensive to run and impossible for most teams to train or adapt. Forge starts from a different premise. For a well-defined use case, the work decomposes into a small number of distinct jobs, and each job is better served by a part built for it.

A forged system has three kinds of parts:

  • A domain world model at the core. It holds the long-term memory of the domain and of the current task, understands the state it is looking at, and predicts what a proposed action would do before the action is taken. It is the organ that judges, and its verdict is what lets the system iterate rather than guess.
  • Organs around it. Each organ owns one step of the end-to-end task: finding where to act, proposing an action, checking it, producing the final output. Organs can be transformers, state-space models, encoders, or small generative models. Nothing requires them to share an architecture, a vocabulary, or a training recipe. This is the inhomogeneous part of the name.
  • Trained connectors. Organs exchange representations, not prose. A connector is a small trained mapping between the output space of one organ and the input space of the next. Connectors are what turn a set of models into one system.

The loop is the other half of the design. The system does not commit to its first proposal. It proposes, the world model predicts the fitness of the proposal, the proposal is revised, and the loop continues until the world model is satisfied or a budget is spent. Memory carries across iterations and across tasks, so the system improves on a repository the longer it works on it.

Three consequences follow, and they are the point of the method. A forged system is small, because each part is sized for one job; the first instance below stays under one billion parameters in total. It is trainable by a small team, because each organ is trained on its own signal with its own data, on one machine. And it is local, because a system of this size runs on a workstation, and in most configurations at the edge.

The coding instance, version 0.0.1

The first forged system is built for software engineering: given an issue written by a person and a repository, produce the change that resolves it, the task that SWE-bench1 made the field's standard. The task decomposes into the loop in Figure 2.

Wherever a published benchmark exists for an organ's job we measure against it, on the same split and the same rule as the published work, so the numbers can be read next to the field's. Where no benchmark fits, because the organ does a job no existing model was asked to do, we define the measurement, state its rule in full, and measure the strongest available models under exactly the same conditions. The Localizer is an example of the first case; the Predictor of the second.

2.2 Localizer

The Localizer is a 137-million-parameter retriever over the functions of a repository, trained on real issue-to-change pairs from an open code retriever5. Its job is to put the functions that the fix will touch at the top of a ranked list. We measure it on SWE-bench Verified1,6, all 500 issues, with every function in the repository as the candidate pool, and count an issue as localized when a function touched by the reference fix is in the top ten.

Forge Localizer, 137 million parameters: 73.8% of issues localized in the top ten on SWE-bench Verified. Training lifted the same base retriever from 34.2% to 73.8%.

The best published function-level localization scores, from the SweRank and SweRank+ papers3,4, are 81.6% accuracy at 10 on SWE-bench Verified (an 8-billion-parameter retriever with a GPT-4.1 reranker; 81.2% with the authors' own 32-billion-parameter reranker) and 89.8% on SWE-bench Lite (8-billion retriever plus 32-billion reranker). Our 73.8% is 7.8 points below the best Verified score and 16.0 points below the best Lite score, at 137 million parameters against 8 to 40 billion, and with a single retriever, no reranker. The published scores use the stricter rule that every function touched by the fix must be in the top ten, on the authors' own function pools. The Localizer is one organ of a loop that can return for what it missed.

2.3 Predictor: the coding world model

The Predictor answers one question: given the issue, the code as it was, and a proposed change, how far is the change from what the issue asks? It reads the three as text and outputs one continuous number: an error, 0 for exactly the fix, rising with how far the change is from the fix, and 1 or more for a change that is not the fix at all. It never runs code and never sees a test. It has 137 million parameters.

What this test measures

Code understanding without any external test. The judge sees only three things: the issue written in plain language, the code as it was, and a proposed change. To score the change it has to relate the goal stated in words to the behaviour of the written code and predict the consequence of the change: whether the program, after this change, does what the issue asks. There is no test suite to run, no execution, no hint about which candidate is real. The ground truth comes from real fixes whose test results are known, and the judge never sees those results. In world-model terms the score is the accuracy of the judge's prediction of what a change does to a program. That prediction is what a coding world model exists to make, and it is the capability this benchmark isolates.

The test

Judge Benchmark v1 uses 100 issues drawn from 813 test-verified GitHub fixes (SWE-bench-family sources1,2,6, held out by repository from all our training data). For each issue the judge scores 9 to 10 candidate changes one at a time: the real fix, a cosmetically altered copy of it, up to four broken versions of it, up to two unrelated commits from the same repository, a fix for a different issue, and an empty change; 938 candidates in total. Input per call: the issue text, the code before the change, and the change itself, up to 8,192 tokens, byte-identical for every judge. Score scale: 0 is exactly the fix, 100 is not the fix. A decision is right when a real fix scores below 50 and a wrong change above 50. The headline is balanced accuracy: the average of the share of real fixes accepted (of 200) and the share of wrong changes rejected (of 738). Language models were called with deterministic settings through their vendors' APIs or run locally at the quantization shown.

How to read it

The Predictor accepts real fixes as reliably as the best model in the test (191 of 200, the same as Claude Opus 5) and rejects broken versions of a fix better than any of the 14 language models, including the two above it. That is the world-model property: predicting what a change does rather than whether it looks plausible. The models fall into two regimes. Lenient judges (DeepSeek V3.2, Qwen3-32B, Llama 3.3 70B) accept the real fix and most broken variants alike; they answer only 0 or 100 and reject fewer than one in ten of the broken versions that change a single token or line. Strict judges (GPT-5.6 Sol, Claude Opus 5, gpt-oss-20b, Gemini) reject half or more of those at the price of rejecting 10 to 26 percent of real fixes. The Predictor is the only judge that combines the lenient group's acceptance of real fixes with the strict group's rejection of broken ones.

2.4 Proposer

The Proposer is the organ that writes. Given the Localizer's targets and the Predictor's verdict on its last attempt, it produces a new candidate change and keeps what it learned in memory, across iterations of one task and across tasks on the same repository. It is a compact module of about 50 million trainable parameters that works in the representation space the other organs share, not in text.

A proof of concept of the full loop has been run: Proposer, memory and judge together, in a controlled setting with a perfect judge standing in for the Predictor. In those early runs the Proposer reached the correct answer through iterative refinement in 85%+ of cases, and it learned to use its memory and the judge's verdicts to revise its proposals rather than restart. These are early, controlled results. Proposal quality on unseen repositories with the trained Predictor in the loop is the measurement that counts, and it is the current work.

2.5 Connector

The Connector is the trained mapping from the accepted proposal to the output: either directly to tokens, or into a small coding language model that writes the final change in the repository's own style. It is the last organ to be built, and the one that turns the loop's internal representation into a change a person can read and a test suite can run.

CHAPTER 3

What this means, and what is next

The attention of the field is on the largest labs and the largest models. The result in Section 2.3 says something different: a specialized model built on a world-model design, for one use case, can reach the level of the best models in that field, and it can be built by a very small team.

The first forged system was built with under $15,000 of compute by two co-founders in the Netherlands. Completing it, to a full software-engineering system near the state of the art, will take an estimated $10,000 to $30,000 more. The finished system will be under one billion parameters.

Three things follow from that size:

  1. Anyone can build one. A system of this size can be designed, trained and iterated for a specific use case on a budget a small team can raise.
  2. It runs where the data is. A sub-billion system runs on a workstation and, in most configurations, at the edge. Sovereign and on-premise deployment stops being a compromise.
  3. Startups can own their model. The path from wrapper to owner: design, train and integrate a system built for your use case, at or near the performance of the largest general models on that use case.

A future that is not only for the largest players

We envision a future in which startups and founders are not held at the stage of building wrappers because they cannot raise billions to train a giant model. We see a future in which any company, whether it supplies software or uses it, can build a complete stack: uniquely designed and trained models, integrated into its own application layer, delivering solutions of real value that are independent of the large players.

Large language models are today's best architecture for broad, general work, and we do not expect that to change. But they are huge, expensive to build and to run, and they lack what long-running autonomous work demands: long-term memory, global planning, and the ability to predict the consequences of their own actions before taking them. For real-world operations that must run unattended, coherently, for a long time, in one specialized field, we believe the answer is different: world-model-based, forged, inhomogeneous systems such as MALWIM, built for each specific use case, that are efficient, long-running, coherent, and within reach of any team.

We see a world in which everyone benefits, the benefits are shared among all who contribute, everyone has a say in what gets built, and the market rewards the best work rather than the deepest pockets.
