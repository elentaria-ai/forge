Forge: Compact World-Model Systems Forged for One Use Case

Khashayar Mansourizadeh1 and the Forge team1

1 Elentaria, Netherlands · Correspondence: khashayar@elentaria.ai

Judge Benchmark v1 (PDF) Results table Cite Announcement

Abstract

Most of the field expects capability to come from ever larger homogeneous models. We take the other road. Forge is a method for building a compact system of specialized parts for one use case, forged around a domain world model that carries long-term memory, understands the domain, and predicts the consequences of actions before they are taken. The parts need not share an architecture; they are connected through trained connectors and each acts as an organ with one job. We call the resulting architecture MALWIM: Multi-Stage Agentic Loops With Inhomogeneous Models.

This note describes the first instance, built for software engineering, and reports the first measured results of its organs. The core organ, the Predictor, is a 137-million-parameter coding world model that scores a proposed code change against the issue it is meant to fix, without running any test. On a benchmark of 100 test-verified GitHub issues and 938 candidate changes, it ranks third of 15 judges by balanced accuracy, tied with Gemini 3.8 Flash and behind Claude Opus 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol, and first of all 15 at rejecting broken versions of a fix. The whole system stays under one billion parameters, runs on a single workstation GPU, and was built by two co-founders for under $15,000.

1Forging and MALWIM

A large language model is one homogeneous network asked to do everything: remember, understand, plan, write, and check. It does all of it adequately and none of it efficiently, and its size makes it expensive to run and impossible for most teams to train or adapt. Forge starts from a different premise. For a well-defined use case, the work decomposes into a small number of distinct jobs, and each job is better served by a part built for it.

A forged system has three kinds of parts:

  • A domain world model at the core. It holds the long-term memory of the domain and of the current task, understands the state it is looking at, and predicts what a proposed action would do before the action is taken. It is the organ that judges, and its verdict is what lets the system iterate rather than guess.
  • Organs around it. Each organ owns one step of the end-to-end task: finding where to act, proposing an action, checking it, producing the final output. Organs can be transformers, state-space models, encoders, or small generative models. Nothing requires them to share an architecture, a vocabulary, or a training recipe. This is the inhomogeneous part of the name.
  • Trained connectors. Organs exchange representations, not prose. A connector is a small trained mapping between the output space of one organ and the input space of the next. Connectors are what turn a set of models into one system.

The loop is the other half of the design. The system does not commit to its first proposal. It proposes, the world model predicts the fitness of the proposal, the proposal is revised, and the loop continues until the world model is satisfied or a budget is spent. Memory carries across iterations and across tasks, so the system improves on a repository the longer it works on it.

Three consequences follow, and they are the point of the method. A forged system is small, because each part is sized for one job; the first instance below stays under one billion parameters in total. It is trainable by a small team, because each organ is trained on its own signal with its own data, on one machine. And it is local, because a system of this size runs on a workstation, and in most configurations at the edge.

2.3 Predictor: the coding world model

The Predictor answers one question: given the issue, the code as it was, and a proposed change, how far is the change from what the issue asks? It reads the three as text and outputs a single number, 0 for the fix and 1 for anything that is not the fix. It never runs code and never sees a test. It has 137 million parameters.

To measure it against the best available judges we built Judge Benchmark v1: 100 test-verified GitHub issues drawn from public SWE-bench-family sources and held out by repository from all our training data. For each issue a judge scores, one at a time, the real fix, a cosmetically altered copy of it, up to four broken versions of it, up to two unrelated commits from the same repository, a fix for a different issue, and an empty change: 938 candidate changes in total. Every judge receives byte-identical inputs of up to 8,192 tokens. The headline metric is balanced accuracy, the average of the share of real fixes accepted (of 200) and the share of wrong changes rejected (of 738), with the decision threshold at the midpoint of the scale.

Three observations from the table. First, the Predictor accepts real fixes as reliably as the best model in the test and rejects broken versions of a fix better than any of the 14 language models, including the two above it; that is the world-model property, predicting what a change does rather than whether it looks plausible. Second, the models fall into two regimes: lenient judges that accept the real fix and most broken variants alike, and strict judges that reject half the broken variants at the price of rejecting real fixes too. The Predictor is the only judge that combines the lenient group's acceptance of real fixes with the strict group's rejection of broken ones. Third, its remaining weakness is specific and known: changes that are valid but address a different issue. That is a training target, not a size problem.

Cost: one full run of the benchmark costs $0 for the Predictor and the local open models on a single workstation GPU, and between $1 and $57 per run for the API models at list prices.

2.4 Proposer and Connector

The Proposer generates candidate changes for the Localizer's targets and revises them under the Predictor's verdict, with memory carried across iterations and across tasks. Its design is fixed and it is in training; we report no number until a held-out measurement exists, and we note that the end-to-end result will be bounded by the localization and prediction errors above. The Connector, the trained mapping from the accepted proposal to output tokens or to a small coding language model, is the last organ to be built.

3What this means, and what is next

The attention of the field is on the largest labs and the largest models. The result in Section 2.3 says something different: a specialized model built on a world-model design, for one use case, can reach the level of the best models in that field, and it can be built by a very small team.

The first forged system was built with under $15,000 of compute by two co-founders in the Netherlands. Completing it, to a full software-engineering system near the state of the art, will take an estimated $10,000 to $30,000 more. The finished system will be under one billion parameters.

Three things follow from that size:

  1. Anyone can build one. A system of this size can be designed, trained and iterated for a specific use case on a budget a small team can raise.
  2. It runs where the data is. A sub-billion system runs on a workstation and, in most configurations, at the edge. Sovereign and on-premise deployment stops being a compromise.
  3. Startups can own their model. The path from wrapper to owner: design, train and integrate a system built for your use case, at or near the performance of the largest general models on that use case.

Next steps, in order: finish the Proposer and the Connector; run the benchmark on all 813 verified issues and add more judges; report the first end-to-end software-engineering result of the complete loop. We will publish each of these here as they are measured, under the same rule as this note: numbers only where an independent, held-out measurement exists.
